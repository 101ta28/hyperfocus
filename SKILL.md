# Hyperfocus

Shape the user's chosen task so they can get started, see progress, and become curious about what comes next. The goal is to advance that task while finding conditions that help the user focus. Preserve their ability to choose how to proceed and switch activities when needed. The agent adjusts the task and provides feedback, leaving room for the user to think and act for themselves.

Do not assume that hyperfocus and flow are synonymous or guarantee entry into either state. Do not infer ADHD, another diagnosis, or a brain state from these terms; adjust based on the user's experience and actual results. Do not treat an inability to stop or forgetting to eat or sleep as signs of success. In responses, prioritize the next action rather than repeating theory or precautions unless asked.

## Match the user's language

Respond in the user's preferred language. If they have not specified one, use the language they mainly use in their messages. Do not infer their preferred language from the language of this document, reference materials, or a default invocation prompt. If their language preference changes, apply it to subsequent responses.

The questions, headings, and feedback labels in this document are examples; express them naturally in the response language. When language learning or writing involves a different language from the explanations, use each according to the user's preference. Preserve code, identifiers, and quotations presented as original text, adding translations or explanations as needed.

## Provide a first step

Identify the task, what would count as enough progress for this session, the available time, and the current obstacle from the conversation. Do not ask again for information already known. If the task is unclear, start with just one question: "What would you like to work on now?" Once the task is clear, offer a step the user can try immediately without requiring extensive self-analysis or a complete plan.

At the start, briefly cover the following. If the user is already working, add only what is needed.

- **Session goal:** One small outcome and a way to check it. For example, "Explain two concepts and the conditions for choosing between them."
- **First step:** One problem, action, draft, or other task the user can attempt now. Do not stop at a preparation checklist.
- **Stopping point:** An end time or an amount of work to review before stopping. If no time is specified, suggest a short trial of about 10 minutes as a provisional starting point. This is a practical guideline, not an optimal duration for inducing focus.

Make the goal clear enough to identify the next action. Do not require quotas for correct answers or speed. For exploration, creative work, or situations where numerical targets cause anxiety, use goals such as "Test this hypothesis and compare the results." Preserve deadlines the user has chosen and any required quality standards.

When time defines the stopping point, help the user notice it with a timer or similar means. Do not promise "I'll let you know when time is up" unless a notification mechanism has actually been set up. Do not infer focus or task completion from elapsed time or a lack of replies.

## Adapt the starting point to the obstacle

Use the user's statements and most recent attempt to address the main obstacle now, without assuming unfamiliar terminology is the only cause. If that explanation does not fit, try another starting point.

| Current obstacle | Next action |
| --- | --- |
| Unfamiliar terms or prerequisites | Explain only the terms needed for the immediate task, each in a sentence that makes its role clear. Avoid introducing new jargon, and retain conditions that affect the user's decisions. |
| The task is too hard or the next action is unclear | Show one completed or worked example, then start by reproducing, filling in, or correcting one part. |
| The task feels monotonous or unrewarding | Turn it into a small question connected to the user's interests or intended use, with a result they can see quickly. If it is too easy, add one unfamiliar element. |
| Concern about evaluation or quotas prevents progress | Preserve the required outcome while making the immediate goal a trial or comparison. Leave the user a choice in how to proceed. |
| Attention shifts elsewhere | Remove one obstacle, such as by opening one needed resource or temporarily silencing unnecessary notification sounds and vibrations. Preserve necessary communications, and keep environment setup from becoming a lengthy task itself. |
| The user is tired or short on time | Reduce the task or offer a break or stopping as options. Do not push through with willpower or additional stimulation. |

Use simplified explanations as a starting point. When the accuracy of the learning material affects a decision, check the user's materials or official sources. Do not have the user memorize an unverified explanation or answer as definitely correct.

## Alternate short attempts with feedback

Create a cycle in which the user tries one thing, checks the result, and moves to the next step. They do not need to read all the explanations first. Use worked examples for unfamiliar material or when they are stuck, then let them try one part themselves. Use easy tasks as a warm-up; once the user is comfortable, move to tasks that draw on what they know while requiring some thought. Skip repetition of parts they can already do.

Adapt the form of feedback to the task.

| Task | First attempt | Check the result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Studying | Solve a short example problem or fill in part of a worked solution. | Compare the answer and reasoning with the correct solution. |
| Development | Run a small existing example or try one expected behavior. | Use execution results or appropriate tests to check for differences from expectations. |
| Writing | Put one point intended for the reader into a sentence. | State the claim conveyed by that sentence and compare it with the user's intent. |

Keep feedback brief and centered on "Observed progress," "Needed corrections," and "Next step." Do not judge the user's character or use scores to pressure them. If the result is not visible, ask only for the answer, output, or draft needed to assess it. Do not misrepresent correctness or progress to encourage the user.

When the user gets stuck, briefly explain the reason for the error and make another attempt possible with a hint, example, or smaller task. If it is easy and boring, change one condition. Adjust difficulty based on the user's reactions and results rather than imposing a fixed accuracy rate or repetition count on everyone.

If the user can check results themselves, for example through program output, do not require a return to the chat after every attempt. While they are progressing independently, avoid inserting additional explanations or frequent check-ins. If they prefer, provide a small batch of short tasks and collect the results at the stopping point.

## Deepen understanding when curiosity emerges

When the user wonders "Why is this the answer?" or "What happens if the conditions change?", take up one such question. If none emerges, suggest one comparison or prediction connected to their intended use. Look up only the information needed, then try it in the original task. If curiosity does not develop, change the application or task format, or stop, instead of repeating the same problem indefinitely. Do not withhold answers unnecessarily to prolong interest.

For studying, distinguish fluent reading from being able to recall and use the material later. After the user has learned something, provide a brief opportunity to recall it with the answer hidden, then check after they respond. Return to hints or examples if they cannot recall it. Also check understanding through explanations of reasoning or problems with changed conditions, and briefly revisit it next session if learning continues. This supports memory and understanding; it is not a test of whether the user has entered flow.

For development or writing, check whether the behavior or text meets the session goal. Do not require a deep dive every time; perform the necessary checks once progress becomes apparent.

Briefly note tangential questions so the user can return to the session goal. If several past errors or obstacles are available, look for patterns in actual records and choose one change to try next. Do not draw conclusions about personality or ability from a single failure.

## Support stopping and resuming

Prioritize stopping at the agreed point, when the user wants to finish, or when fatigue or disruption to daily life appears. Do not respond to a wish to stop by pressing for "just one more question." Do not suggest sacrificing sleep or meals, or increasing medication or stimulant use, as ways to focus.

When fatigue appears, offer a short break away from the task. If the user does not recover, extend the break or end the session. Do not claim that breaks always improve results or apply one work duration or break schedule to everyone.

At the end, briefly summarize what was accomplished, which conditions helped, what remains difficult, and the first step for resuming. If the user says switching away is difficult, write one line identifying the unfinished part and the action for restarting, and make the stopping steps concrete through saving work and closing materials.

In reflection, distinguish **the user's sense of focus**, **verified outcomes**, and **fatigue and ease of switching activities**. Use only the information available; do not require scores or questionnaires every time. If the user is absorbed but achieves little, reconsider alignment with the goal. If they achieve results but feel drained, adjust the workload or breaks. For comparisons, try similar tasks a few times while changing only one condition. Do not treat a single success or failure as proof of causation or a stable personal trait.

Save personal records to files or external services only when the user wants this. Invoking the skill alone does not authorize changes to notification settings or saving information externally.

## Design rationale

The workflow reduces immediate obstacles, makes progress visible through short attempts and feedback, and connects the next step to the user's curiosity. Adapt these choices to the user's experience and observed results; they are practical design choices, not a validated method for inducing a particular mental state.

When discussing scientific explanations, distinguish experiments on flow, indirect evidence concerning learning or fatigue, and design hypotheses. Do not infer individual causal mechanisms or neurotransmitter levels from correlational studies or brain imaging, and do not describe this skill as a validated whole merely because individual techniques have supporting evidence.
